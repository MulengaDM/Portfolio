Finding out which quotes a business wins

Workplace pilot, 2026
UK industrial procurement and export business

The problem

The company's accounting system only records sales it wins. Nobody could say how many quotes turned into orders, which suppliers lost most often, or whether business was lost on price or delivery time. My manager asked for a lost-sales database that needed almost no manual input.

What I did

  • Extracted every quote line from 900+ Excel quote files with Python, reading each cell of a fixed template.
  • Matched each quote line to sales orders by part number, close descriptions and a 120-day window, then cross-checked against the job-costing sheets.
  • Merged hundreds of different spellings of supplier names into clean records.
  • Spotted that quotes from before the data began were distorting the win rate, and excluded them.
  • Built an offline dashboard that answers five management questions and rebuilds with one click when new data arrives.
  • Designed a 25-table SQL Server database to bring the company's scattered data into one place for reporting.

What it showed

For the first time, the business could see which suppliers and parts it wins and loses most often, and how much quoting effort each customer takes. The analysis also showed a gap: nobody recorded why a quote was lost, so I recommended adding that field.

Tools Python (pandas, openpyxl), SQL Server, Excel, HTML dashboard

No company data, client names or figures are shown, and the code stays private.

Does social media move Bitcoin?

Final-year dissertation, 2025
University of Portsmouth, graded 70%

The question

Crypto traders talk about FOMO (fear of missing out) and FUD (fear, uncertainty and doubt). I tested whether that mood on Twitter, especially from accounts with huge followings, actually changes Bitcoin's price, trading volume or volatility.

What I did

  • Collected 3.2 million tweets from February to June 2022 through the Twitter API.
  • Labelled each tweet as FOMO, neutral or FUD with a RoBERTa language model trained on Twitter text.
  • Weighted each tweet by the author's follower count, then built daily and four-hourly sentiment indices.
  • Tested the indices against returns, volume and volatility with Granger causality, GARCH-family models and an event study around major news.

What I found

Day to day, sentiment had almost no link to returns (correlation about 0.03). Within four hours there was a weak signal, and around major news, spikes in sentiment came with unusual price and volume moves. Twitter moves Bitcoin only under certain conditions, not all the time.

Tools Python, pandas, NumPy, NLTK, Hugging Face Transformers, Matplotlib, Seaborn, Google Colab

Predicting a shopper's next purchase

University pair project, 2024–25
Based on the RecSys Challenge 2022

The problem

Most visitors to a fashion shop are new or change their taste quickly, so there's no purchase history to go on. The challenge was to predict the item someone buys using only what they looked at in one session.

What we did

  • Cleaned and filtered the Dressipi session dataset and turned views into implicit feedback.
  • Handled brand-new items with candidate filtering, so they could still be recommended.
  • Trained an item-based collaborative filtering model (k-nearest neighbours) and benchmarked it against a random baseline.
